\section{Scope boundaries: full table}\label{app:limits}

\paragraph{What RULER measures here: synthetic answers AND short decodes.}
RULER is adverse to this method in two independent ways, and they damage
different quantities.

\emph{Where the accuracy goes, classified by what the generator varies.}
Table~\ref{tab:rulerclass} sorts the thirteen tasks by the parameters RULER's
own generator sets. The loss is not spread across ``multi-key'' tasks and is
not a property of needle retrieval: it sits in the \emph{two} cells whose
haystack is itself made of key--value pairs, and it more than doubles again
when those pairs are UUIDs. Every other task --- including
\texttt{niah\_multikey\_1}, which plants four needles, and
\texttt{niah\_multiquery} and \texttt{niah\_multivalue}, which ask for four
answers --- is within noise. Multiplicity alone costs nothing.



The mechanism follows from the classification. Tier~1 selects on a residual: it
\emph{seeks} outliers, and it keeps the needle in every one of these tasks.
Tier~2 orders archived rows by a rank-$r$ projection fitted to the archive's own
content, and that is the step the two losing cells break. When the haystack is
prose, the planted pair is an outlier against it and the fitted basis separates
them. When the haystack \emph{is} key--value pairs, the bulk the basis is fitted
to and the row it must find are the same distribution, so there is no direction
left in which the target stands out; when the pairs are UUIDs, there is no
linguistic structure for either tier to use. The measurement agrees: at answer
steps the sketch omits the context's top-scoring row on a fifth of
\texttt{multikey\_3} records, mis-ordered by seven scaled logits --- a bias in
the order the sketch induces, not a threshold narrowly missed.

\emph{So the honest scope of the accuracy claim} is not ``multi-key retrieval
degrades''. It is: where a rank-$r$ sketch must separate rows drawn from the
same distribution as the bulk it was fitted to, it cannot, and the more those
rows are stripped of natural-language structure the worse it gets. That is a
real limitation of this design and it would recur outside RULER wherever a
context is composed of near-identical records --- a log file of UUIDs, a key
store, a table of hashes.

\emph{Short decodes cost speed, and this is the method's shape rather than an
accident of RULER.} The recall tier fits $z_p$ on the request's own decode
queries, so until it has enough of them it serves a \emph{provisional} index:
identity basis, gate fully open, $z=Z_{\max}$. That is the most conservative
rung available, not a guess --- it over-fetches and falls back to dense --- so
the warm-up spends throughput and never recall. A RULER answer is about
fourteen steps and never amortises it: 81\% of its scans run provisional, and
it falls back on $0.305$ of scans against $0.002$ for a long decode at the same
64k context. A fallen-back lane attends densely and is exactly correct, so what
the short decode loses is speed, not answers.

The price of a warm-up that returns nothing is measurable, and LongBench~v2
measures it on real documents: those questions are scored with
\texttt{max\_tokens}${=}1$, one prefill and one token, so the request pays the
setup in full and has no decode to recover it in. Dense takes \lbKDWall{}
minutes over the \lbKDN{} questions and vestigekv \lbKVWall{}, a cost of
\lbKCost{}\% against a \lbKVSpread{}\% spread between two vestigekv runs. That
is the floor: a one-token answer is the worst case this design has, and it
costs about one percent.

\emph{One corpus, two protocols, and they are not one experiment.} LongBench~v2
enters this paper twice and the two numbers must not be read as one. As a
\emph{benchmark} it supplies \lbKDN{} multiple-choice questions, each scored
with \texttt{max\_tokens}${=}1$: that is the quality result above, and the
warm-up floor beside it. As a \emph{corpus} it supplies novels --- the Literary
and Detective sub-domains, the most natural text in the set, each truncated to
an exact context length --- which the timing runs continue for 4096 tokens with
\texttt{ignore\_eos}. Different protocol, different arms, different quantity:
one prices the warm-up where nothing amortises it, the other prices the steady
state where a long decode does. Choosing novels is the conservative choice
rather than the flattering one, because at matched context and decode length
prose falls back \emph{more} than random tokens ($0.483$ against $0.407$), so a
speedup that survives there is not an artefact of synthetic input.

\emph{The three factors stack, but not on the same quantity.} Three adverse
factors meet in RULER: content planted so as not to belong to its context, a
context composed of competing near-tied records, and a fourteen-token answer.
The first two cost \emph{accuracy} and compound --- \texttt{multikey\_3} is the
one cell where both are extreme and is the worst result in the paper at
\rulerKDeltaNiahMKc{}. The third costs \emph{throughput} and, said plainly,
slightly \emph{helps} the accuracy: a short answer keeps the index on the
$Z_{\max}$ clamp, which over-fetches and loses the top archived row on 16.0\%
of answer-step records against the fitted certificate's 22.85\%, so a
longer-decoding RULER would have shown a marginally wider gap. Drop any one
factor and its cost goes: real documents (LongBench~v2, the same answer to
every one of \lbKDN{} questions), prose instead of a haystack of key--value
pairs (the other eleven tasks, within $2\sigma$), or a long decode (fallback
$0.483\to0.0032$ on the same 64k of real text). What the paper cannot show is a
case where all three hold and the method is unaffected: RULER \emph{is} that
case, and it is \rulerKDelta{} behind.

That this warm-up is paid in throughput rather than in answers is what
separates it from the families it will be compared against, where the
least-informed window is the one in which what to discard is decided
irreversibly; Table~\ref{tab:related} sorts them on that axis.

None of this is an excuse, and the obvious excuse is false as measured: the
ordering above is not by how natural the text is --- prose is the worst case,
not the easiest --- but by decode length, which moves the rate $151\times$ where
text type moves it $1.6\times$ and in the wrong direction. The honest scope is
narrow: the accuracy gap is real, its mechanism is identified, it is bounded on
real documents by the LongBench~v2 result, and it is unclosed.
